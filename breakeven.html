Breakeven Employment

How many jobs are needed to hold the unemployment rate steady

Payroll growth only lowers the unemployment rate when it runs faster than the labour force is growing. The pace that exactly absorbs new entrants, leaving the unemployment rate unchanged, is the breakeven rate of job growth, and it is just the growth rate of the labour force. Because net immigration has slowed sharply, labour-force growth, and so breakeven, has fallen toward zero. The catch is that the published labour force jumps every January when BLS resets its population controls, so those resets have to be stripped out first.

In Jun 2026, payrolls ran at +111 on a three-month average against an estimated breakeven of -151 a month, so hiring is above breakeven, implying downward pressure on the unemployment rate.

How breakeven has moved

Breakeven is the trend pace of labour-force growth, which fell through the slow-recovery 2010s, rose sharply during the 2022–24 surge in population growth, and has since dropped back toward zero as net immigration faded. The gap between payroll growth and labour-force growth below is what drives the unemployment rate.

Methodology

The change in the unemployment rate is modelled as an Okun-style bridge:

\[\Delta_{12} u_t = c + \rho\, \Delta_{12} u_{t-12} + \beta\,\big(\Delta_{12}\log E_t - \Delta_{12}\log E^{*}_t\big) + \varepsilon_t\]

where E is payroll (CES) employment and E* is breakeven employment, which is the labour force. Breakeven cannot be a free latent series here: for any β a breakeven path fits the equation perfectly, so it would be unidentified. It is instead supplied as an input, the labour force, so breakeven jobs per month is the current pace of labour-force growth.

Population-control adjustment. The household-survey labour force jumps every January when BLS resets its population controls (e.g. about +2.1m in 2025, −1.1m in 2026). These are not flows of workers in that month: they recognise immigration that arrived earlier and then extrapolate it forward through the year. So the adjustment is not to erase each jump (that would delete the immigration and understate the surge years) but to credit it back as growth over the months it actually accrued — a multi-year window for the large 2025 census re-benchmark (which caught up several years of undercounted immigration), the prior year for ordinary annual controls. The result is a continuous labour force whose growth carries the surge where it happened and unwinds it as immigration faded.

We then fit a smooth-trend unobserved-components state-space model to that series and read its trend slope as the current pace of labour-force growth, which is breakeven. We read the slope, not a 12-month trailing average, because the trailing window lags badly when growth turns quickly. The trend’s flexibility is fixed to a small value so it reads as a smooth pace rather than chasing the noisy month-to-month survey; the band comes from the smoother’s own uncertainty (wide at the end, where the current pace is least pinned and the latest CPS readings are volatile). The unemployment rate is used as-is, since population controls scale the unemployment level and the labour force together and cancel in the rate.

The change-in-unemployment equation itself is estimated as a state-space model (SARIMAX with MA(11) errors, the correct structure for overlapping 12-month differences, with native missing-data handling). It yields β and the model’s implied path for Δ12 u; the breakeven number does not depend on β.

One caveat: the dependent variable is an overlapping 12-month difference, so its errors are serially correlated by construction; the MA(11) error model accounts for this. The breakeven series still inherits the noise of the household survey, which is why the band, while tighter than a raw rolling estimate, at present comfortably includes zero. Because this measure is faithful to the reported labour force, which has been contracting sharply as immigration reverses, its latest reading runs more negative than projection-based external estimates that smooth through the recent survey prints; read the level as directional (breakeven has fallen well below zero) rather than precise.
